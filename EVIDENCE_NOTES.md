# Evidence Notes

The seven PDFs in `references/` are the source of truth for the practical guides. These notes distinguish evidence about communication and engagement from broader clinical outcomes.

## Egan et al. (2010)

**Citation:** Egan, M., Bérubé, D., Racine, G., Leonard, C., & Rochon, E. (2010). Methods to enhance verbal communication between individuals with Alzheimer's disease and their formal and informal caregivers: A systematic review. *International Journal of Alzheimer's Disease, 2010*, 906818. https://doi.org/10.4061/2010/906818

- **Purpose/population:** Review experimental studies intended to improve verbal communication between people with Alzheimer’s disease and formal or informal caregivers; 13 studies met inclusion criteria.
- **Findings:** Memory aids paired with specific caregiver training emerged as potentially effective for topic maintenance and communication. Evidence was restricted by small samples and methodological limitations.
- **Practical recommendations:** Use personalized external supports; train the communication partner rather than expecting the person with dementia to carry the interaction; give focused cues and support attention.
- **Limitations:** Heterogeneous, often weak studies; conclusions about other activity-based approaches were limited.
- **Supports:** starter guides, caregiver guide, familiar objects/photos, Passion Packs, situation guides.

## Morris et al. (2018)

**Citation:** Morris, L., Horne, M., McEvoy, P., & Williamson, T. (2018). Communication training interventions for family and professional carers of people living with dementia: A systematic review of effectiveness, acceptability and conceptual basis. *Aging & Mental Health, 22*(7), 863–880. https://doi.org/10.1080/13607863.2017.1399343

- **Purpose/population:** Review communication-training interventions for family and professional carers.
- **Findings:** Training can improve knowledge and use of communication strategies; evidence for effects on the person with dementia, caregiver burden, and broader psychosocial outcomes was less conclusive.
- **Practical recommendations:** Use structured training, active practice, multiple components, person-centered communication, and reinforcement over time.
- **Limitations:** Considerable intervention and outcome variation; risk of bias; limited long-term follow-up.
- **Supports:** both starter guides, situation guides, caregiver handover/reflection, cards.

## Folder et al. (2024)

**Citation:** Folder, N., Power, E., Rietdijk, R., Christensen, I., Togher, L., & Parker, D. (2024). The effectiveness and characteristics of communication partner training programs for families of people with dementia: A systematic review. *The Gerontologist, 64*, 1–19. https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/gnad095

- **Purpose/population:** Review 30 studies describing 27 family communication-partner programs, involving 671 family members.
- **Findings:** Programs generally improved communication skills and knowledge; behavioral and psychosocial results were mixed. Qualitative work suggested better conversations and attitudes.
- **Practical recommendations:** Teach specific strategies, provide supported practice and feedback, design for real-life use, and involve people with dementia and families in development.
- **Limitations:** Study quality varied; few telehealth programs; intervention reporting and consumer involvement were inconsistent.
- **Supports:** starter booklets, caregiver operations, reflection, online sessions, cards.

## Harris et al. (2025)

**Citation:** Harris, C., Webber, R., Livingston, G., & Beeke, S. (2025). What are the communication guidelines for people with dementia and their carers on the internet and are they evidence based? A systematic review. *Dementia, 24*(3), 577–594. https://doi.org/10.1177/14713012241292486

- **Purpose/population:** Assessed 39 eligible websites, 164 communication strategies, and their correspondence with systematic reviews and practice guidance.
- **Findings:** Advice clustered around supporting strengths, valuing interaction, emotional safety, working together, adapting to context, knowing the individual, and focusing on broader meaning. Only 61% of identified strategies appeared in systematic reviews or formal practice guidance.
- **Practical recommendations:** Avoid rigid universal rules; adapt to the individual, context, current priority, strengths, and preferences.
- **Limitations:** Presence in evidence sources is not proof of effectiveness; online guidance quality and rationale varied.
- **Supports:** all four modes, especially situation guides and careful evidence wording.

## Houghton et al. (2022)

**Citation:** Houghton, C., Hennessy, M., Smyth, S., Hennelly, N., Smalle, M., Jordan, F., Jones, C. H., Quinn, M., Casey, D., & Teahan, Á. (2022). The experiences and perceptions of young people and older people living with dementia of participating in intergenerational programmes: A qualitative evidence synthesis. *Dementia, 21*(7), 2144–2171. https://doi.org/10.1177/14713012221112385

- **Purpose/population:** Synthesized qualitative experiences of young people and older people living with dementia in intergenerational programs.
- **Findings:** Programs could enable connection, enjoyment, learning, changed perceptions, and reciprocity. Relationship building, preparation, appropriate activities, continuity, and facilitation mattered.
- **Practical recommendations:** Prepare young people, create opportunities for mutual contribution, personalize shared activity, allow relationships time to grow, and avoid framing older adults only through dementia.
- **Limitations:** Diverse settings and programs; some included studies had methodological limitations; qualitative findings do not establish clinical effects.
- **Supports:** young-adult starter, reciprocity language, activities, Passion Packs, session continuity.

## Lu et al. (2021)

**Citation:** Lu, L.-C., Lan, S.-H., Hsieh, Y.-P., & Lan, S.-J. (2021). Effectiveness of intergenerational participation on residents with dementia: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Nursing Open*. https://doi.org/10.1002/nop2.919

- **Purpose/population:** Reviewed 13 articles on intergenerational participation among long-term-care residents with dementia.
- **Findings:** The synthesis reported improved pleasure and reduced disengagement, but no apparent improvement in quality of life, depression, or overall engagement measures.
- **Practical recommendations:** Design programs around enjoyable participation and reduced disengagement; choose suitable activities and support both generations.
- **Limitations:** Small, heterogeneous evidence base spanning 1993–2015; results do not establish broad clinical benefit.
- **Supports:** young-adult guide, signs of engagement, activity guides, cautious outcome claims.

## van der Steen et al. (2025)

**Citation:** van der Steen, J. T., van der Wouden, J. C., Methley, A. M., Smaling, H. J. A., Vink, A. C., & Bruinsma, M. S. (2025). Music-based therapeutic interventions for people with dementia. *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, Issue 3, CD003477. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD003477.pub5

- **Purpose/population:** Updated Cochrane review of professionally delivered music-based therapeutic interventions for people with dementia.
- **Findings:** Effects varied by outcome and time point; certainty ranged and longer-term evidence was limited. This evidence concerns therapeutic programs, not ordinary music sharing.
- **Practical recommendations for Sage & Seed:** Use personally meaningful music as a voluntary conversation and engagement support; watch response, manage volume, and stop or change when uncomfortable.
- **Limitations:** Heterogeneous interventions, settings, and measures; findings should not be transferred directly to informal conversation activities or framed as treatment.
- **Supports:** music activity guide and careful distinction between shared music and therapy.

## Cross-paper practice principles

Person before diagnosis; communication-partner adaptation; one idea at a time; sufficient response time; emotional safety; attention to nonverbal communication; personalized visual or memory supports; meaningful mutual participation; flexibility; reflection on what worked. The guides focus on communication, connection, dignity, and engagement—not treatment, cognition, depression, or quality-of-life claims.
